This is a free, public library about Kenya's 15 pastoralist counties, run by the ASAL Humanitarian Network (AHN). It gathers evidence that pastoralism is the backbone economy of the drylands, and that herders turn changing rain and pasture into milk, meat, trade and healthy land.

Studies from the last 30 years, court cases and laws, community land titles, carbon projects, county budgets and the Equalisation Fund, and who funds what in each county.

ASAL-Pastoralist Hub

Pastoralism is the backbone economy of Kenya's arid and semi-arid lands. Let's build the evidence that shows it.

These 15 arid and semi-arid counties are too often described by drought and scarcity. Yet pastoralism contributes about 10% of Kenya's GDP and half of its agricultural GDP. Herders turn variable rain and pasture into milk, meat, trade and healthy rangelands: variability is a resource to work with, not a failure to fix. This hub brings together 30 years of research, court cases, carbon projects, community land titles and county funds, so everyone can see what is known, what is contested, and where pastoral green economies can grow.

Equalisation Fund: what is owed and what arrives

Article 204 of the Constitution gives the fund 0.5% of national revenue every year, to bring water, roads, health facilities and electricity in marginalised areas up to the national standard. Far less has actually been paid in.

Where the debate stands

  • Refocus on the original 14 counties: pressed by those counties' governors, and by the Treasury Cabinet Secretary, who told Parliament the expansion to 34 counties was a mistake.
  • Keep the wider reach: supporters say the 2018 policy corrected anomalies by reaching neglected communities wherever they live.
  • Scrap the fund: the Treasury Cabinet Secretary has also proposed ending it, arguing the money is spread too thin to matter.
  • Pay what is owed: the Controller of Budget reports at least KSh 48bn still owed, delaying projects in counties such as Turkana, West Pokot, Wajir, Narok, Samburu, Mandera, Garissa, Baringo and Marsabit.

Is low county output used to decide where money goes?

Not directly. The Fourth Basis for sharing revenue among counties, approved for 2025/26 to 2029/30, gives each county its baseline allocation, shares KSh 2 billion equally among 11 identified counties, and splits any additional money by population (45%), an equal share (35%), poverty (12%) and geographical size (8%). Gross County Product is not one of the measures.

The Equalisation Fund is different. Article 204 of the 2010 Constitution sets aside 0.5% of national revenue each year to bring water, roads, health facilities and electricity in marginalised areas up to the level the rest of Kenya enjoys. The Constitution names no counties: it leaves the criteria to the Commission on Revenue Allocation. CRA's first policy (2013) named 14 marginalised counties, 11 of them in this hub: Turkana, Mandera, Wajir, Marsabit, Samburu, West Pokot, Tana River, Narok, Garissa, Isiolo and Lamu, plus Kwale, Kilifi and Taita Taveta.

The second policy (2018) changed the unit from counties to sub-locations, spreading the fund across 1,424 areas in 34 counties, mainly by access to education, water, sanitation and electricity. Critics argue this thins out money meant for the historically marginalised counties. CRA began reviewing the policy in mid-2026, and Article 204 lapses 20 years after the Constitution took effect, around August 2030, unless Parliament extends it.

So low output helps a pastoralist county only indirectly, through the poverty and land-area weights. One case to make in the policy review: reward investment in growth enablers such as rangeland health, water, market access and mobility routes, not only measures of deprivation.

Sources: Senate motion on the Fourth Basis · The Star on the second marginalisation policy · The Star on the 2026 review

Why invest in the drylands

Kenya’s farmland cannot feed 84 million people alone. The drylands, 89% of the country, can be Kenya’s protein basket and its next growth frontier, if we invest in them on pastoral terms.

On pastoral terms. “Food basket” must not mean turning rangelands into irrigated crop schemes; large schemes such as the Tana Delta Irrigation Project fell short and cost herders land. What works is investing in mobility, communal land, herder-managed rangelands, animal health and livestock markets. That is also where young people’s jobs are: along the livestock value chain.

Answers to common objections

  • "Pastoralism is a backward, low-value way of life." Valuation studies find official statistics undercount pastoralism’s market and non-market value, and Kenya’s own ASAL Policy (2012) and Range Management and Pastoralism Strategy 2021–2031 commit to supporting it. Sources: Nyariki and Amwata 2019; IUCN 2007; ASAL Policy; Strategy 2021–2031.
  • "Herds and mobility cause overgrazing." Research traces the overgrazing story to colonial-era narratives that still shape how degradation is read, and rangeland management by herders shows high returns. Sources: Little 2025; KEFRI.
  • "Settling herders and big irrigation schemes are the way forward." Large schemes such as the Tana Delta Irrigation Project fell short, while mobility supports nutrition and resilience. Sources: Lebrun 2009; Tufts 2023.
  • "Drought relief is enough; investing ahead is too costly." Early action is far cheaper than late relief, and climate disasters already cost Kenya about 2 to 2.8% of GDP a year. Sources: LEGS 2018; Climate Policy Initiative.
  • "Pastoral markets are informal and unreliable." Camel milk trade run largely by women moves milk reliably from the rangelands to city markets. Sources: Nori 2023.
  • "Money sent to these counties is wasted." Much of the money owed has never arrived: the Controller of Budget reports at least KSh 48bn still owed to the Equalisation Fund. The hub follows each shilling from allocated to audited, and an audit query is not a finding of wrongdoing. Sources: Equalisation Fund tracker; Partners and investment.

Feedlots and fodder: feedlots work best as part of the market chain, buying animals early in a drought and finishing them for sale, owned by or linked to herders. They should not take grazing land or replace mobility. Fodder bought ahead of drought keeps the core breeding herd alive.

How the scenario works, and its assumptions

"Green" here means a county’s pastoral economy grows and holds up through drought: livestock value up, drought losses down, mobility kept and rangelands healthy. The scenario asks how much investment in the nine sectors that takes. Each county moves up four steps: Starting, Building (a third of the way), Green-ready (two thirds) and Green.

What is needed is set per person, then raised each year for rising climate risk. A, the pastoral economy: $1,200 per person by default, which is Kenya's climate plan cost of about $62 billion for 2020 to 2030 shared across roughly 50 million Kenyans. B, catching up on basic services: a draft $1,000 per person for the panels to agree. In the north and north-east, World Bank figures put electricity access at 7%, safe water at 45% and improved sanitation at 36%. Population comes from the county figures in "County economies".

What it earns back: each dollar invested in the pastoral economy is assumed to add about 20 cents a year to county output (editable below). That is cautious next to the evidence above: returns of 24 to 1 for herder-run rangeland management and 41 to 1 for early off-take. Basic services are not counted as earning anything, though they do.

Who pays: 1, each county’s budget share, based on its 2025/26 equitable share (KSh 117.2 billion for the 15 counties, National Treasury, June 2025); 2, the 2025/26 Equalisation Fund amounts already in the hub (not yet found for Isiolo, Tana River, Lamu, Elgeyo Marakwet and Laikipia); 2, the national government, development partners, climate funds, the private sector and disaster risk finance cover the rest. The scenario does not yet count returns from the investment itself, so it is cautious.

Criteria and assumptions are a draft for AHN's Research and Funding panels to agree. Boundaries: IEBC county boundaries, via mikelmaron/kenya-election-data. Climate plan cost: Climate Policy Initiative, Landscape of Climate Finance in Kenya.

Where the data comes from, and how names are shown

  • Humanitarian funding: OCHA's Financial Tracking Service. Development and climate funding: IATI, and the World Bank, African Development Bank, IFAD, Green Climate Fund and Adaptation Fund portals.
  • Public money: National Treasury, the Commission on Revenue Allocation, Parliament, the Controller of Budget and the Office of the Auditor-General.
  • An amount is shown only where it is published. When a project covers several counties without splitting the money, it is marked "covers this county" with no county amount.
  • An organisation is named only where a public document names it, with the link. Otherwise it is shown by type, such as "a local or national NGO", until it gives consent. The hub is never the first to name anyone. A reviewer can hide a name for safety, especially for local peace actors.
  • Audit entries copy the audit opinion and figures from the report. They are audit queries, not findings of wrongdoing; check for the county's response. Entries that interpret findings need two reviewers, and entries naming individual officers also need the administrator's safety check.

How it works

Search and suggestions bring items in, the right panel approves them, and only approved records count.

  1. Search, plus a 30-year backfillEvery Monday morning (Nairobi time), Claude searches court decisions, carbon registries, gazettes, county funds, university repositories, journals and the news for the 15 counties. A daily backfill works back from 2023 to 1996, three years at a time.
  2. Anyone can add knowledgeAnyone with the link can comment on any record or item. People invited as contributors can also send suggestions and proposed edits through a form.
  3. The administrator routes itEach item goes to one of three panels: Research (studies), Policy and legal (cases, laws, land) or Funding (investments, county funds, audits). The administrator can assign items to particular reviewers.
  4. Approval by tierTier 1, such as a published law or figures copied from an official report: shown in the register straight away, marked "not yet checked", and confirmed by one reviewer. Tier 2, such as interpretations, bias or friendly labels and edits: two of three reviewers. Tier 3, such as anything naming individuals: two of three plus the administrator's safety check. Any reviewer can raise a tier; only the administrator can lower one.
  5. Public sources and namesFunding data comes only from public sources. An organisation is named only where a public document names it, or with its consent.
